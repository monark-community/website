## From Web1 to Web3

Web1 (1990s to early 2000s) was read-only. Pages were basic, static HTML with little interaction, and information flowed one way, from publishers to readers, through clear gatekeepers.

Web2 (mid-2000s to today) is read-write. Social media, cloud services and platforms like YouTube let everyone create content as well as consume it. The price was a concentration of power and business models built on exploiting user data.

Web3, still emerging, adds ownership: read, write, own. It promises to reshape online relationships with blockchains, decentralized networks and token-based ecosystems where governance is shared and users own their data. Is it living up to that promise?

## Promise and Reality

### Ownership Comes with Responsibility

Web3 advocates call true digital ownership revolutionary, but the reality is more nuanced. Blockchains do let people own digital assets directly, without intermediaries. That ownership also brings responsibility and technical complexity that most users aren't ready for. Seed phrases, private keys and wallet management add a lot of friction, and when people lose access to their assets forever because of a simple mistake, it's fair to ask whether this "feature" helps mainstream adoption.

### Users as Stakeholders, in Theory

Web3 is right to criticize the way Web2 giants extract value from user data. In theory, tokens let users share in the value they help create. In practice, it often looks more like speculation than real participation. Many projects hand out tokens in ways that heavily favor early investors and founders, repeating or even amplifying the inequality they claim to fix. When 70 to 80% of tokens are reserved for insiders, is this really a new model?

### Community Governance

DAOs (decentralized autonomous organizations) are a bold experiment in community governance. Most of them struggle with low participation, voting power that follows wealth, and governance attacks. Community-led projects often end up run by a small group of large token holders, far from the democratic revolution that was promised.

## Where Web3 Delivers

Despite these criticisms, Web3 brings real innovation in several areas:

- moving value by code, without intermediaries
- credible neutrality in financial applications
- tamper-proof record-keeping for critical infrastructure
- new ways for distributed teams to coordinate
- transparency for systems that used to be opaque

## How Monark Approaches It

As an incubator that connects academia and industry, Monark takes a pragmatic approach to Web3:

- Start from real problems. The most successful Web3 projects fix genuine pain points instead of forcing blockchain where it isn't needed.
- Make it simple. Mainstream adoption needs a far simpler user experience that hides the complexity and keeps the benefits.
- Design fair tokenomics. Token systems should align incentives for everyone involved, not just enrich the first participants.
- Work with regulation. Ignoring legal frameworks isn't sustainable, so we work within evolving rules rather than trying to get around them.
- Mix models. The best Web3 implementations will probably combine centralized and decentralized parts, chosen for each use case rather than for ideological purity.

Web3 is a significant step in internet technology. It isn't the utopia its supporters describe, and it isn't the outright scam its critics see; the truth sits somewhere in between. At Monark, we want to build Web3 applications that last and deliver real value, which takes intellectual honesty, technical excellence and a willingness to drop ideological positions when they clash with reality. We expect the future to combine the best of each generation of the web, in systems built around human needs rather than technological dogma.

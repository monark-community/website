## Smart contracts

Smart contracts work well when several parties need to transact without fully trusting each other, when the conditions can be programmed and checked on-chain, and when everyone benefits from seeing the terms.

They struggle with complex legal terms that need interpretation, with anything that depends on real-world data (the "oracle problem"), and with mistakes, since an immutable contract can't be fixed or adjusted.

## Transparency and immutability

Blockchain is a good fit for supply chain tracking where several parties need verified information, for public records that must not be tampered with, and for financial systems where audit trails are essential.

It's a poor fit when privacy comes first (even with the progress of zero-knowledge proofs), when data volumes and throughput needs exceed what blockchains can handle today, or when compliance requires changing or deleting records.

## Digital ownership and provenance

It works for tokenized real-world assets with clear legal frameworks, for digital art and collectibles where provenance matters, and for cross-border transactions where traditional systems add friction.

It works less well for assets whose on-chain ownership has no clear legal recognition, when transaction speed is critical, or when a transaction costs more than the value it adds.

## When not to use a blockchain

We work in Web3, and we still have to admit that a blockchain is often unnecessary when:

- a centralized database would do (faster, cheaper and more efficiently);
- the parties already trust each other;
- the problem doesn't need decentralization, immutability or trustlessness;
- users won't put up with the friction of today's blockchain apps;
- regulations require central control or the ability to reverse transactions.

## Challenges that remain

- User experience: wallet management, transaction fees and confirmation times still keep most people away.
- Scalability: throughput is still limited, even with layer 2 solutions.
- Energy: Proof of Stake has improved efficiency, but the total impact of networks is still a concern.
- Regulation: inconsistent rules around the world create real business risk.
- Complexity: the learning curve is still steep for developers and users.

## How Monark decides

Our university and industry collaborations focus on practical uses. We evaluate each use case carefully before recommending blockchain, favor projects where it brings a unique and measurable advantage, are honest about technical challenges, and take regulation into account from day one.

Blockchain can change a lot in specific areas, but it isn't a universal answer. The best projects will be the ones where decentralization, immutability and trustlessness add more value than they cost. That's the test we apply to the projects we incubate.

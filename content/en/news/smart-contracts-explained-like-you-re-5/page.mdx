## What is a smart contract?

A smart contract is an agreement that carries itself out: when certain conditions are met, it performs the actions written into it. Think of a vending machine. You put in a coin, pick a snack and get it, with nobody behind the counter.

More technically, a smart contract is code deployed on a blockchain such as Ethereum. Once deployed, it runs exactly as programmed, so the parties need neither an intermediary nor trust in each other.

> Smart contracts are neither particularly "smart" nor legal "contracts" in the traditional sense. They're deterministic programs that automatically execute when triggered by specific blockchain transactions.

## Three examples

### Uniswap

Uniswap is a decentralized exchange that runs entirely on smart contracts. The contracts hold pools of tokens (liquidity pools) and let people swap one token for another with no order book and no central operator. The exchange rate is calculated from the ratio of tokens in each pool.

It has real limits. Liquidity providers face impermanent loss (value they can lose when token prices move), gas fees (the cost of a transaction) climb when the network is congested, and the contracts remain exposed to certain exploits despite several security audits.

### NFTs

NFTs (non-fungible tokens) use smart contracts to prove who owns a digital asset and how scarce it is, which gives artists and creators new ways to earn money from digital work.

The NFT market has also been hit by wash trading (people trading with themselves to inflate prices), copyright infringement and environmental concerns. Many NFT contracts ([see Smilesssvrs, Boss Beauties, MekaApes](https://pinata.cloud/blog/hundreds-of-millions-of-dollars-of-nfts-are-still-unprotected/)) don't even store the artwork on-chain. They point to centralized servers that could disappear.

### DAOs

A DAO (decentralized autonomous organization) uses smart contracts to make group decisions and allocate resources. Members usually vote with governance tokens, and a proposal executes automatically once it reaches a set threshold.

In practice, many DAOs have low turnout, are controlled by their largest token holders and sit in a legal grey zone. The "[The DAO](https://www.coindesk.com/learn/understanding-the-dao-attack)" hack in 2016 led to Ethereum's contested hard fork and showed what happens when code that can't be changed has a flaw.

## What smart contracts can replace

Smart contracts can take over work usually done by trusted institutions: escrow, insurance claims, supply chain checks and even parts of lending. That can lower costs and remove gatekeepers.

The catch is that a contract only knows yes or no, while real situations often call for judgment. When something unusual happens, a person has to step in, which undercuts the whole idea of an autonomous contract.

In software, smart contracts move applications from servers controlled by one company to decentralized apps (dApps), where processing and governance are spread across a blockchain network. That makes scaling, user experience and upgrades harder. A deployed contract is notoriously difficult to update, which becomes a security risk as soon as someone finds a vulnerability.

## The uncomfortable parts

On the technical side:

- Deploying and using smart contracts is still expensive on most layer 1 blockchains (the base networks, such as Ethereum).
- Contracts inherit the limits of their blockchain, such as throughput and latency.
- Feeding real-world data into a contract reliably is hard. This is called the "oracle problem".
- Code vulnerabilities have cost DeFi protocols billions in lost funds.

In practice:

- Most users can't verify a contract's code, so they end up trusting a "trustless" system.
- Whether a smart contract is legally enforceable varies widely from one jurisdiction to another.
- Immutability is a feature until you need an upgrade.
- Building smart contracts is still harder than traditional software development.

## Where we stand

Smart contracts could change how people deal with each other online, but they are no cure-all. At Monark, we look for specific cases where they have a clear advantage over traditional systems instead of putting blockchain everywhere. We expect the best uses to complement existing software and legal frameworks rather than replace them, pairing automation with the flexibility people and institutions bring. That's the approach we take in our Web3 incubator programs and university collaborations, because the real world rarely fits neatly into deterministic code.
